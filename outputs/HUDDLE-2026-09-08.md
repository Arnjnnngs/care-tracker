# Team huddle — 2026-09-08

Aaron: *"all team members should have a team huddle and bring ideas. whichever of the top 3 are
beneficial, you bring it to me and the benefit and I'll tell you go or no go."* And: *"you should
look at some of the most successful businesses to see how their workflows are set up to be efficient
with their teams."*

Each seat brought what it would change about how this work runs. Fourteen ideas; the three below are
the ones that would have prevented something that actually happened today or this week.

---

## THE TOP 3 — each needs a go / no-go

### 1. One release, both apps, same day — as one job rather than two

**Who raised it:** the Builder and the Scribe, independently.

**The problem it fixes, from today.** The medication-purpose work was built for care-tracker, and
ChemoWell was a separate job started afterwards. In between, care-tracker's auditor found **four
defects** — the box that could not be cleared, the wording frozen into the record, the crash from a
medication named "Constructor", and two clinically wrong sentences. Every one of them existed in the
ChemoWell build too, and each had to be carried across by hand. One of them I nearly missed.

The same thing happened structurally before: ChemoWell had a copy-review role for months that
care-tracker did not, and nobody noticed until a bad sentence shipped.

**The change.** A feature that belongs in both apps is ONE piece of work with two outputs. Same
patch shape, same tests, same audit brief, shipped in the same session. The auditor reviews both
diffs together, so a finding lands in both places at once instead of being remembered into the
second one.

**The benefit:** roughly half the audit cost for two-app features, and it structurally removes the
"fixed in one, forgotten in the other" failure — which is the failure that produced this project's
worst release, when a bug fixed in ChemoWell in v68 was never carried across and shipped to Brandi
in care-tracker.

**Cost:** none. It is a change in sequencing, not in headcount.

---

### 2. A written "what could this destroy?" line before any code is written

**Who raised it:** the Zero Day Auditor — the seat with the best record here, five correct blocks.

**The problem it fixes.** Every expensive release on this project was built, then audited, then
rebuilt. v69 was built three times. v74 today was built three times. The auditor is finding real
things, but it finds them **after** the build, which is the most expensive moment to find them.

The one release that did NOT go that way was v52, where the write model was stated in advance and
two traps were caught before a line existed. That is the whole evidence base, and it points one way.

**The change.** Before any release that touches a record or the medication list, one short paragraph:
what this writes, what it deletes (the answer should be nothing), what happens if the user does the
destructive thing twice, and what happens if it fails halfway. It goes in the commit and the auditor
reads it first. **Five minutes, no agent, no tokens worth counting.**

**The benefit:** the auditor stops spending its budget rediscovering the shape of the change and
spends it attacking the change. On today's evidence that is one rebuild saved per release.

---

### 3. Aaron gets ONE artifact per release, not a conversation

**Who raised it:** the Scribe and the Voice.

**The problem it fixes, in his words:** *"I'm doing too much micro managing."* He has had to ask
twice for the Enhancer list, ask what "S" meant, ask who is actually on the team, and ask whether
work was duplicating another chat. None of that is his job, and all of it came from information
arriving as prose scattered through many messages.

**The change.** Every release produces one page, in this order, and it is the only thing he has to
read: **what changed for Brandi · what needs his phone · the proposal list with sizes · what is
outstanding · the evidence, last.** Technical detail moves below the fold or into the repo. If it
does not fit on a tablet screen without scrolling past the important part, it is written wrong.

**The benefit:** he stops reconstructing state from a conversation. It also makes the Enhancer list
impossible to bury, which is the specific thing he complained about today.

**Cost:** none. It is the same information in a fixed order.

## What the huddle deliberately did NOT propose

- **More roles.** Every seat agreed the headcount is right. The independent auditor and the
  mechanical checks are earning their keep — six correct blocks this week, four of them today.
  What has been failing is delivery and sequencing, and a new seat fixes neither.
- **Handing the coding to a subagent.** Argued and rejected before (Rule 2.2); today's evidence
  supports that again — every defect the auditor found was a CONTEXT mistake (a guard the file
  already had, a clinical implication, a check that could not fail), and a fresh coder makes more of
  those, not fewer.
- **A second auditor.** One cross-check that bites beats two that agree.

## On how good teams actually run, since Aaron asked

The three above are the same three things that show up in every well-run engineering group, stated
without the jargon:

1. **Fix a class of bug everywhere it lives, in one go** — not one instance at a time.
2. **Write down what you intend to break before you break it** — cheaper than finding out after.
3. **One owner-facing artifact per unit of work** — so the person paying for it does not have to
   assemble the picture themselves.

None of them costs money. All three are sequencing.
